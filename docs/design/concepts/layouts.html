Where the neurons and the tries live

Three directions for the page, to fix two problems: the workbench covers the replies, so you can't watch a reply change while you work on neurons; and each Run replaces the steered reply, so earlier tries are lost. Wireframes only; the text and values are made up.

Direction A

Workbench first, replies docked

The workbench becomes the page, laid out like Dolittle: tries in a rail on the left, the deck in the middle, the inspector on the right. The two replies sit in a strip under the deck and stay in view, so a Run streams there while the deck stays put.

Gains

  • Everything at once: pins, neurons, replies, tries.
  • Closest to Dolittle's rail | main | inspector.
  • A try in the rail can show its own neurons and move the point to its word (problem 5).

Costs

  • Replies get a short strip; a long reply means scrolling it.
  • The deck gets less height than the approved concept.
  • Busiest first screen for someone who just wants to ask a question.
Direction A · drawer

Workbench first, the inspector slides over

Direction A without the fixed inspector column. With nothing selected, the deck and the reply strip take the whole width beside the tries. The front layer's strongest neurons stay as a line across the top of the deck; clicking one, or a neuron on the grid, slides the inspector in from the right, over the deck's right edge, with Set, Scale × and Zero. The Inspector button opens it on whatever is selected; Esc, × or clicking empty grid slides it away. The deck shifts left while it's open, so the front card is never covered.

Gains

  • The deck gets the concept's full width most of the time.
  • The reply strip is wider, so more of each reply shows.
  • The inspector appears where your attention already is: on the neuron you just clicked.

Costs

  • The strongest line holds five or so neurons across the deck's width, fewer than the eight the inspector lists today.
  • The drawer covers the right of the reply strip while open, unless it stops at the deck.
  • Opening and closing is motion; under reduced motion it would appear and disappear without sliding.
Direction B

Reading first, workbench beside

The page stays the conversation, and the workbench opens as a split on the right half instead of covering everything. Tries are tabs over the steered reply: Try 1, Try 2, Try 3, so you can flip between them against the reply.

Gains

  • The question and replies stay the main thing; the workbench is there when you want it.
  • Smallest change from today's page.
  • Tabs keep tries without a new panel.

Costs

  • Each half is narrow: two reply columns plus a deck in one window.
  • Tabs show one try at a time; comparing two tries means flipping.
  • The inspector shrinks under the deck.
Direction C

Tries first, workbench as a drawer

The page becomes a stack of tries. The reply sits at the top; each Run adds a try under it, newest first, with its pins and branch word, so every attempt stays readable at once. The workbench is a drawer from the bottom over half the window, so the newest try streams above it.

Gains

  • Every try readable at once, each labelled with what changed.
  • Reads like a lab notebook: question, then attempts.
  • The drawer can close to give tries the whole page.

Costs

  • Tries stack top to bottom, so the reply and a try are no longer side by side line for line.
  • The deck gets half the height, wide but short.
  • A long list of tries needs its own scrolling and clearing.
